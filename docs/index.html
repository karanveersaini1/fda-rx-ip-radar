    FDA in the Federal Register

    The Federal Register is the government's official daily journal — FDA rulemaking, advisory committee meeting notices, and comment periods all publish here first. For a practitioner it is the forward calendar: what the agency is about to do, as opposed to the approvals feed, which records what it just did.

    The legal machinery, in one page

    why an FDA approval is also a patent event
    Orange Book listing — 21 U.S.C. § 355(b)(1)
    An NDA holder must list every patent that claims the drug or a method of using it. Listing is what links the FDA's regulatory act to the USPTO's property right — and it is the dataset this site joins.
    Paragraph IV certification — § 505(j)(2)(A)(vii)(IV)
    A generic applicant's assertion that a listed patent is invalid or not infringed. Filing one is a statutory act of infringement (35 U.S.C. § 271(e)(2)) that lets the brand sue immediately.
    30-month stay — § 505(j)(5)(B)(iii)
    If the brand sues within 45 days of a Paragraph IV notice, FDA may not approve the generic for 30 months (or until the generic wins). Litigation timing is therefore built into the statute.
    NCE exclusivity & the "NCE-1" date
    A new chemical entity gets 5 years during which no ANDA may even be submitted — except a Paragraph IV challenge may be filed at year 4. That fourth anniversary ("NCE-1") is the starting gun for patent litigation, and it appears as a marker on the timelines.
    180-day generic exclusivity — § 505(j)(5)(B)(iv)
    The first Paragraph IV filer earns 180 days as the only generic — the bounty that makes challenging weak patents worthwhile.
    Method-of-use patents & the "skinny label" — § viii
    A generic can carve a patented indication out of its label instead of challenging the patent — which is why patent type (composition of matter vs. method of use) changes litigation strategy.
    Patent term extension — 35 U.S.C. § 156
    Regulatory review time can be restored to one patent per product (up to 5 years). FDA approval literally changes a patent's expiration date.
    The BPCIA "patent dance" — 42 U.S.C. § 262(l)
    Biologics have no Orange Book: patent lists are exchanged privately with biosimilar applicants. The public cannot compute a biosimilar-entry date — a transparency gap this site surfaces rather than hides.

    What this site does

    methodology, for readers who bill by the hour

    Every FDA drug approval is also a property-rights event: it activates listed patents as statutory gates on competition, starts exclusivity clocks, and opens or closes litigation windows on dates you can compute. RxIP Radar watches the interface between the two agencies where that happens — FDA and the USPTO — and republishes itself every morning. Nothing here is estimated or scraped from commercial databases; every number traces to an official government file.

    1. Pull the approvals A scheduled job queries openFDA's Drugs@FDA endpoint each morning for every approval action since January 1 — original approvals (NDA, BLA, ANDA) and post-approval supplements alike.
    2. Pull the patent estates The same job downloads the FDA Orange Book bulk files: the patents and exclusivity periods every NDA holder is required to list under 21 U.S.C. § 355(b)(1), refreshed by FDA monthly.
    3. Join on the application number The NDA/ANDA number is the statutory key linking both datasets. From the join, the site computes each product's runway — last patent expiry, exclusivity end, and the NCE-1 date when Paragraph IV challenges may first be filed.
    4. Publish as a static page No server, no database, no tracking. The site is regenerated and re-deployed daily by an automated workflow; the raw JSON behind every view is publicly inspectable at data/.

    Why the join is legally sound — and where it isn't

    The Orange Book is not a courtesy database; it is a statutory disclosure. Section 355(b)(1) obliges the NDA holder to list every patent that claims the approved drug or a method of using it, and § 505(j)(2)(A)(vii) obliges every generic applicant to certify against exactly that list. So the patent data here is the same list that controls real ANDA litigation. Two honest caveats, though: listings are self-reported by the brand and FDA performs no substantive review of them (over-listing is policed only by delisting counterclaims and the FTC), and biologics have no public list at all — under the BPCIA, patent positions surface only inside the private § 262(l) exchange. Where the data ends, this site says so rather than interpolating.

    Questions it can answer

    • Portfolio watch: which competitor estates formed this quarter, and how many patents wide are they?
    • Entry planning: for a given brand, when does the ANDA door open — the exclusivity bar, the NCE-1 challenge date, the last listed patent?
    • Evergreening signals: which products keep adding efficacy supplements and later-listed formulation or method-of-use patents?
    • Market openings: which brands just lost their wall — every ANDA approval marks a barrier that expired, was carved around, or fell to challenge?
    • Agency calendar: what is FDA about to do — rulemaking, guidance, advisory committee meetings — via the Federal Register tab?

    Limitations — read before relying

    • Biologics gap: BLAs show no patents because none are publicly listed (BPCIA § 262(l)). Shown as a gap, by design.
    • Listing lag: sponsors have 30 days to file Orange Book listings, so the newest approvals may show zero patents while the estate is still forming.
    • Pending extensions: § 156 patent term extensions in prosecution are invisible until granted and listed — real runways can be longer than shown.
    • No litigation layer yet: Paragraph IV certifications, PTAB inter partes reviews, and district-court dockets are the roadmap, not the present.
    • Unvalidated source: openFDA ships with its own disclaimer; this is research and education, not legal advice and not a substitute for a freedom-to-operate opinion.

    About this project

    from a spreadsheet to a pipeline

    This site began as a manual habit: copying new items off the FDA's website by hand into a Google Sheet, then working through each row one at a time — looking up the drug, its sponsor, its patents, and what the approval actually changed. That workflow topped out at a few items an evening, and most of the hours went to collection rather than analysis.

    RxIP Radar was built to close that gap, as a project for a university ethics course: the pipeline now does the gathering — from official government sources, on a daily schedule — so the human hours go where they belong, to analyzing what each action means. It also supplements a longer-standing passion for the place where law and the life sciences meet, where a molecule's story is inseparable from the statutes wrapped around it.

    Built and maintained by Karanveer Saini — a life-science graduate applying to law school, on the theory that the best way to learn the legal architecture of pharmaceutical competition is to build a machine that watches it move. Source code, ETL pipeline, and raw data available on request.

    RxIP Radar is a student research and education project. It is not legal advice, and no data here should inform medical or investment decisions. Approval data: openFDA (Drugs@FDA). Patent & exclusivity data: FDA Orange Book (patents listed under 21 U.S.C. § 355(b)(1); biologics have no equivalent listing requirement under the BPCIA — that gap is shown, not hidden). Regulatory documents: Federal Register API. Patent links go to Google Patents. Data refreshes daily via GitHub Actions; openFDA itself notes its data is not validated for clinical use.
